Matrix Construction, Encoding, Optimisation and Readout}

\begin{abstract}
We study the use of Gaussian Boson Sampling (GBS) as a trainable generative
model for one-dimensional classical distributions discretised into bit strings.
Starting from the variational WAW framework, we identify and correct a chain of
distinct bottlenecks: a biased initial adjacency-matrix construction, a
representation-hostile bit-string encoding, an unreliable optimisation loop, an
uncontrolled photon-number budget, and the choice of detector (threshold vs
photon-counting). For each we give the physical motivation, the method, and the
measured effect on the Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence between the learned and
target distributions. The combination of a per-pair/parity construction, a
Gray-code encoding, an analytic keep-best optimiser, and photon-number matching
reduces the KL by roughly a factor of three relative to the baseline on smooth
targets. We further show that the residual error on multimodal targets is an
expressivity floor of a single Gaussian state, and that the detector choice
should be matched to the target's concentration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem statement}

A GBS device with threshold detectors produces click patterns $s\in\{0,1\}^m$
whose probabilities are fixed by a symmetric adjacency matrix $A$ through the
torontonian. In the variational (WAW) approach of Banchi
\textit{et~al.}~\cite{banchi2020}, $A$ is first built from training samples and
then refined by trainable per-mode weights $W$, $A\mapsto WAW$, to minimise the
KL divergence to a target distribution. We take as target a smooth
one-dimensional density $p(x)$ discretised into $K=2^{m}-2$ bins (dropping the
all-zero and all-one patterns), and encode each bin as a click pattern.

This report documents a sequence of improvements to that pipeline. Throughout,
quality is measured by the KL divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{target}}\|
p_{\mathrm{model}})$, evaluated either from Monte-Carlo samples or, where noted,
exactly from the analytic click distribution. Three smooth targets are used
(normal, log-normal) together with a bimodal target and, later, sharp
(Cauchy) and asymmetric-bimodal targets.

\section{Background: the factorial/parity bias}

For a zero-mean Gaussian state the photon-number probability is
\begin{equation}
P(\mathbf{n})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det Q}}\,\frac{|\mathrm{Haf}(A[\mathbf{n}])|^{2}}
{\prod_i n_i!},
\end{equation}
where $A[\mathbf{n}]$ repeats row/column $i$ exactly $n_i$ times. The hafnian of
an odd-dimensional matrix vanishes, so any contributing configuration has an
even total photon number. A threshold detector reports only the support
$s_i=\mathbb{1}[n_i\ge 1]$, so the click probability sums $P(\mathbf{n})$ over
all configurations with a given support. Two consequences drive the entire
report: (i) every \emph{odd}-click pattern must double one mode to reach an even
photon count, incurring a $1/2!$ suppression absent for even-click patterns;
(ii) the collision configurations ($n_i\ge 2$) that threshold detection folds
into a click are exactly the information a photon-counting detector would
resolve.

\section{Improving the initial construction}

\subsection{Corrected construction}
The original \texttt{A\_from\_samples} adds diagonal self-loops only for
single-click samples. The first correction (\texttt{A\_from\_samples\_corrected})
counts a self-loop for every clicking mode and boosts the diagonal by
$\sqrt{2}$ to compensate the two-photon bunching term
$P(n_i{=}2)\propto |A_{ii}|^2/2!$. This is motivated only by the $k=1$ case.

\subsection{Parity rule}
The same $1/2!$ factor appears for \emph{every} odd-click pattern, not only
$k=1$. The parity rule (\texttt{A\_from\_samples\_parity}) therefore weights
each sample by $w_s=\sqrt{2}$ if $k=|s|$ is odd and $1$ if even, applied to all
self-loops and edges it touches. It reduces to the $\sqrt{2}$ diagonal boost for
$k=1$ and corrects edge counts from odd-click samples that the diagonal-only fix
leaves biased.

\subsection{Per-pattern weights}
Summing over all photon configurations under a uniform reference
$A_0=\alpha J$ gives closed-form per-$k$ weights
$w_k=R(2)/R(k)$ with
$R(k)=\alpha^{-k_{\min}}\sum_{N}[(N-1)!!]^2\alpha^N f_k(N)$ and
$f_k(N)=[x^N](e^x-1)^k$ (\texttt{A\_from\_samples\_perpattern}). Empirically
this over-suppresses high-$k$ samples (e.g.\ $w_3\approx0.07$), discarding real
pair-correlation information, and was abandoned.

\subsection{Per-pair normalisation}
The failure above is a counting artefact: a $k$-click sample deposits into
$\binom{k}{2}$ edges and $k$ loops, so high-$k$ samples flood the matrix. The
per-pair construction (\texttt{A\_from\_samples\_perpair}) divides each sample's
contribution by the number of entries it touches,
$A_{ii}\mathrel{+}=w_s/k$ and $A_{ij}\mathrel{+}=w_s/\binom{k}{2}$, keeping the
parity weight on top. It eliminates the spurious high-click peak and was the
best initial construction across targets and mode counts
(Fig.~\ref{fig:perpair}).

\subsection{Photon-number (variance) matching}
Rescaling fixes only the \emph{mean} photon number; the variance of the total
photon number, and hence of the click count $k$, is uncontrolled. The
variance-aware construction (\texttt{A\_from\_samples\_variance}) matches the
full click-count histogram $h(k)=\Pr(|s|{=}k)$ by iterative proportional
fitting,
\begin{equation}
\rho_k \leftarrow \rho_k\cdot\mathrm{clip}\!\Big(\big(h_{\mathrm{data}}(k)/
h_{\mathrm{model}}(k)\big)^{\alpha},\,0.1,\,10\Big),
\end{equation}
scaling each shell's contribution by $\rho_k$. The prefactor $\alpha$ tunes the
strength ($\alpha=0$ recovers per-pair, $\alpha=1$ is full matching); different
targets prefer different $\alpha$.

\section{Encoding: Gray code}
The default encoding maps bin index $b$ to its \emph{standard binary}
representation, which is not Hamming-smooth: adjacent bins can differ in all $m$
bits (e.g.\ $01111\leftrightarrow10000$). A smooth $p(x)$ then becomes highly
non-smooth in pattern space, which a pairwise (hafnian) model cannot represent.
A reflected Gray code $g(n)=n\oplus(n\gg1)$ makes consecutive bins differ in one
bit (\texttt{gray\_encoding}). This is the single most effective change: on the
multimodal target it roughly halves the KL, and on the normal target the
initial-construction KL drops from ${\sim}0.4$ to ${\sim}0.25$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gray}).

\section{Optimisation}
The baseline WAW uses random initialisation, a fixed step count, and returns the
last iterate; on Gray-encoded data this collapsed onto a single dominant mode.
Two fixes are used. \texttt{train\_WAW\_bestkl} initialises weights at zero,
monitors the KL each step, and returns the best iterate. More importantly,
sampling with \texttt{torontonian\_sample\_graph} silently rescales $A$, so the
optimised and evaluated distributions differed and the reported KL could
\emph{increase} after training. \texttt{train\_WAW\_analytic} removes this by
minimising exactly the analytic KL (evaluated with the loop torontonian over all
$2^m$ patterns), with keep-best return, so the KL after training never exceeds
the initial value. All later comparisons use this analytic evaluator.

\section{Displacement (parity at the state level)}
Squeezed vacuum contains only even photon numbers, biasing threshold-click
parity. Three ways to break this were explored (from earlier work).
\texttt{train\_displaced\_gbs} trains the full displacement vector
$\boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}$ jointly with the weights, using the loop
torontonian; it removes the parity constraint at the state level but scales only
to small $m$. \texttt{train\_waw\_then\_displace} is a scalable surrogate: WAW
first, then a single scalar displacement $\alpha$ applied to all position
quadratures, tuned by binary search so the odd-parity fraction matches the data.
\texttt{train\_parity\_mixing} is a purely classical workaround: train separate
even- and odd-parity circuits (flipping one mode to map odd data to even) and
mix them with the data's parity ratio.

\section{Photon-number-resolving readout}
A counting detector reads $\mathbf{n}$ directly, with
$P(\mathbf{n})\propto|\mathrm{Haf}(A[\mathbf{n}])|^2/\prod_i n_i!$; for a
collision-free pattern this is simply $|\mathrm{Haf}(A_S)|^2$ over the support
submatrix --- the clean signal the construction heuristics were estimating
(\texttt{pnr\_readout}). Marginalising the exact PNR distribution reproduces the
threshold distribution, and quantifies the discarded mass: ${\sim}30\%$ of the
probability sits in collision patterns at $m=4$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:pnr}). However, at fixed modes PNR adds no matrix parameters, and
under a probability-matched encoding it does \emph{not} generally beat threshold
learning: its usable outcomes are dominated by a steep photon-number magnitude
hierarchy. Its genuine value is (i) as a diagnostic, and (ii) that its model
family is intrinsically more \emph{concentrated}, which helps on
sharply-peaked targets (Sec.~\ref{sec:diag}).

\section{Honest evaluation: encoding from samples}
The probability-matched encoding (\texttt{rank\_assign}) places the largest
target mass on the most-probable outcome. Using the \emph{true} target to build
it is an oracle; in practice only samples are available. The realizable version
(\texttt{empirical\_target\_from\_data}) estimates the target shape by decoding
samples back to bin indices and histogramming, and uses that empirical estimate
for both the encoding and the training objective. The true target is used only
to report a generalisation KL. Empirically the two KLs coincide (no overfitting
of sampling noise), and the empirical encoding matches the oracle to within
${\sim}0.01$ KL, confirming the encoding is robust.

\section{Diagnostic findings}
\label{sec:diag}
\paragraph{A central spike and its suppression.} On smooth unimodal targets the
learned distribution shows a spike at the peak bin, roughly twice its neighbours
at $m=5$ and $2.8\times$ at $m=6$. It is not a histogram artefact
($\hat p$ is smooth at the peak); it is the model's single most-probable pattern
($q_0/q_1\approx2.3$) mapped onto the peak bin, which forward KL is content to
overshoot. Reverse-KL regularisation does not help --- the concentration is
structural for the low-parameter weight family. The effective control is the
mean photon number: the default $n_{\mathrm{mean}}=m/2$ over-squeezes, and
lowering it to ${\approx}2$ (\texttt{train\_threshold\_nmean}) flattens the head,
removes the spike, and even lowers the KL on broad targets.

\paragraph{Detector choice matches target concentration.} The threshold model
family is comparatively spread (higher entropy, lower top-$k$ mass), while the
PNR family is concentrated. Consequently threshold wins on broad targets (the
normal, ${\sim}42/62$ effective bins occupied at $m=6$) and PNR wins on
concentrated ones (the asymmetric bimodal, ${\sim}26/62$ occupied), robustly
across seeds. The practical rule is to match the readout to the target's
concentration.

\section{Conclusions and outlook}
The GBS distribution-learning pipeline has several orthogonal bottlenecks, each
individually addressable: the initial construction (parity / per-pair /
variance), the encoding (Gray), the optimiser (analytic keep-best), the photon
budget (mean via $n_{\mathrm{mean}}$, variance via click-count matching), and
the detector (threshold vs counting). Their combination reduces the KL by about
a factor of three on smooth targets. The residual error on multimodal targets is
an expressivity floor of a single Gaussian state --- neither objective
reweighting nor encoding removes it. The natural next step is added expressivity:
displacement (already prototyped) or a small mixture of GBS states, evaluated
under the honest, sample-only protocol and the analytic KL used here.
